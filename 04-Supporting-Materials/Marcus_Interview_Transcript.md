With the **Investment Team**, the friction is around "context switching." They’ll bring us into a portfolio company with very little lead time, and I’ll have to spend a week just figuring out what the actual problem is because the initial "ask" was so vague. There’s no standard for how they "hand off" a portfolio company to us, so we spend a lot of time retracing steps. And with **IT**, it’s just the sheer amount of back-and-forth. Getting security clearances or data access shouldn't take three weeks of emails, but right now, there’s no "fast track" for Intelligence pod projects. We’re treated like any other internal request, even though our work is tied to external deadlines and portfolio success. It’s just a lot of administrative friction that eats into our actual building time.

**Question:** How do you coordinate with those teams today—through regular forums/cadences, named counterparts, or primarily ad hoc?

**Answer:** It’s almost entirely **ad hoc**. I don’t have a standing meeting with Sarah, and I don't have a regular sync with the Investment Partners. It’s all "pull-based"—I reach out when I have a problem, or they reach out when they have a fire. With **Sarah**, it’s a lot of "Hey, checking in on this" emails. It works because we’re small enough that I can keep it all in my head, but it’s definitely not a system—it’s just me being a human router.

**Question:** As DI grows, which of those interfaces most needs to become more structured?

**Answer:** Without a doubt, the **Investment Team interface** and the **Compliance/Legal process**. As we grow, we can't have thirty people all Slacking random Partners. We need a formal "Front Door" for the Investment team—a clear process for how they request our help, what information they need to provide, and how we communicate the status back to them. We need a "named lead" or a liaison on their side so I’m not re-educating every new Principal on how my pod works. And the **Compliance interface** needs to move from "ad hoc emails" to a "predictable pipeline." We need a dedicated resource or at least a dedicated "Intelligence block" of time from Sarah’s team. If we’re going to be shipping software at scale, we need a service-level agreement (SLA) for reviews. "Submit by X, get feedback by Y." Without that, we’ll never be able to manage portfolio company expectations. If we don't fix those two, we’re just going to be a bigger, more expensive version of the chaos we have now. We’ll be doing more work, but it’ll feel three times as hard to get it out the door.

## DI Operations role

It’s a total waste of time for someone who should be focused on product strategy. That kind of "project coordination" shouldn't fall on a full-stack dev. Then there's the **manual scoping and re-scoping**. Because we don't have a formal intake process, I’m constantly having these "discovery" meetings that could have been handled by a simple form or a brief. I’m basically acting as a part-time project manager, a part-time account manager, and a part-time admin. If I could hand off the "chasing," the "reporting," and the "scheduling," my pod could probably ship 20-30% more than we do now. It’s all the "meta-work" that’s killing us.

**Question:** Where do you find yourself acting as the "glue" because there isn't an obvious owner?

**Answer:** I’m the "glue" for basically every **cross-functional handoff**. When a project needs a legal review, I’m the one pushing it through. When a portfolio company is slow to respond, I’m the one nudging the Investment Partner to nudge the CEO. When Priya’s team and my team are out of sync, I’m the one trying to bridge the gap. So I’m the one setting up the initial calls, explaining our security protocols, and figuring out who their technical lead is. It’s all the "connective tissue" between the high-level investment and the actual technical build. Basically, if it’s not "writing code" or "building an ML model," it usually lands on my plate by default. I’m the one making sure the project doesn't just fall into the cracks between the different pods and the broader Deerfield org. It’s a lot of "emotional labor" and follow-up that keeps the lights on, but it’s definitely not the best use of my time.

**Question:** If you had an Operations leader supporting DI, where could that person most materially improve your team's ability to execute?

**Question:** The biggest thing? Give me a **shield**. I need someone who can sit between me and the constant stream of "urgent" requests from the investment team. If you could handle the initial intake, vet the requests, and help me say "no" (or at least "not yet") using actual data about our capacity, that would be a game-changer. Beyond that, I need you to be the **"Traffic Controller" for dependencies.** You’d be the one looking at my roadmap and Priya’s roadmap and flagging, "Hey, Marcus needs this pipeline in three weeks, but Priya’s team is booked—let's resolve this now." If you could manage that cross-pod coordination and the "chasing" of Sarah for compliance reviews, you’d be eliminating the two biggest bottlenecks that stall our projects. We’re a fast-moving team, and the last thing we need is a bunch of mandatory meetings for the sake of having meetings, or a 20-page "project charter" for every small tool we build. And finally, I still want to keep the **direct relationship with the portfolio company's technical leads**. Does that make sense? I need you to clear the road, not tell me how to drive the car.

## Scaling

**Question:** As your pod grows, what becomes materially harder to manage?

**Answer:** The biggest thing is going to be **quality control and technical debt**. Right now, since it's just me and two others, I can practically see every line of code and every model architecture. But at double or triple the size, I won't be able to "gut-check" everything. If we don't have a structured way to maintain standards, we’re going to end up with a bunch of "special snowflake" projects that are impossible to support long-term. Also, **team morale and burnout** become much harder to track. Right now, I can tell if my engineer is stressed just by his tone on Slack. With a larger pod, it’s way easier for someone to quietly burn out because they’re stuck in that cycle of constant pivots and scope creep. The "chaos" we have now is manageable with three people who are tight-knit, but with ten people, that same chaos just turns into a toxic work environment where nobody feels like they're winning.

**Question:** What works today because the organization is small that won't work at 30?

**Answer:** *"Management by walking around"** is the first thing to go. Right now, I can solve a cross-pod dependency by just grabbing Priya for five minutes. At thirty people, that doesn't work—you can't just "wing it" on coordination. You need a system, or things just don't happen. The **informal prioritization** also dies. David can't be the final arbiter for every single request when there are thirty people's worth of work to manage. We’ll need a more decentralized way to make decisions, which means we need clear frameworks that everyone understands. And finally, the **lack of a "front door"** for the investment team. Right now, they all know me and Priya. At thirty people, they won't know who to go to, and if they keep pinging everyone individually, the noise will be deafening. We’ll spend all our time answering "quick questions" and zero time actually building. The "everyone knows everyone" culture is great, but it’s not a scaling strategy.

**Question:** What operational capability or mechanism do you think DI needs to put in place before it feels necessary?

**Question:** We need a **formal Capacity Planning model** right now, even though it feels like overkill for ten people. We need to start tracking how long things *actually* take—not just my gut feel—so that when we’re at thirty people, we have a baseline to say "no" to a project because we know we’re at 110% capacity. If we don't start that habit now, we’ll never have the data to defend our team’s time later. We also need a **standardized "Portfolio Company Engagement Model."** A simple, repeatable process for how we intake, scope, and deliver a project. It should be a "plug-and-play" system so that when a new Pod Lead starts, they don't have to reinvent the wheel. If we wait until we're at thirty people to define how we work with the rest of Deerfield, we’ll be trying to change the tires while the car is going 80 miles an hour. And honestly, we need a **centralized "Knowledge Base."** Not just for code, but for the "business logic" of what we’ve built. Why did we choose this data source? What were the compliance hurdles for that med-device tool? If we don't start documenting that "tribal knowledge" now, we’re going to waste a huge amount of time re-learning the same lessons as we grow.

**Question:** Conversely, what do you think we should deliberately keep informal or lightweight as we scale?

**Question:** We should keep the **technical decision-making** as lightweight as possible. I don't want a "Technical Review Board" that has to approve every new library or architecture choice. We should trust the Pod Leads and their engineers to make those calls. As long as we’re hitting the shared standards, the "how" should stay with the people doing the work. I’d also want to keep the **culture of "direct access."** While we need a "front door" for *requests*, I don't want to lose the ability for a data scientist in my pod to talk directly to a data scientist in Priya’s pod to solve a problem. We need structure, but not *hierarchy*. The low-ego, "let's just fix it" vibe is the best thing about Deerfield, and I’d hate to see that get buried under layers of "official" communication channels. Basically, keep the **coordination** structured, but keep the **collaboration** informal. We need a map and a schedule, but we don't need a permission slip for every step we take. Does that distinction make sense? It’s a fine line to walk.

## Prioritization

**Question:** Of everything we've discussed, what are the one or two operational issues you think matter most? Why those versus the others? How would you know six months later that we’d actually fixed it?

**Question:** If I have to boil it down, the two that are absolutely critical are **Cross-Pod Coordination** and **Project Intake/Prioritization.** The reason these two matter most is that they’re the foundation for everything else. If we can't coordinate with Priya’s team, we can't ship reliably. And if we can't control the intake, we’re just a "feature factory" that’s constantly burning out. Everything else—like the compliance delays or the lack of documentation—is just a symptom of these two core failures. Six months from now, I’d know we fixed it if: 1. **The "Surprise" Factor is gone:** I shouldn't be finding out about a data pipeline delay on the day I’m supposed to demo a tool. If we have a shared roadmap where dependencies are flagged weeks in advance, that’s a win. 2. **I have a "Data-Backed No":** When an Investment Partner comes to me with a "quick ask," I want to be able to pull up a dashboard and say, "We’re at 95% capacity; if we take this on, the Rare Disease project slips by two weeks. Do you want to make that trade-off?" If the conversation shifts from "vibes" to "trade-offs," we’ve won. Honestly, if I can look at my team in six months and see they aren't context-switching every two days, and I'm not spending half my life in Slack status updates, I’ll know the Ops role was a success. It’s about giving us the space to actually be the "Intelligence" pod instead of just a "Firefighting" pod.

**Question:** What would happen if we did nothing about them over the next 12–18 months? What, if anything, would actually break as DI approaches 30 people?

**Answer:** If we do nothing, we’re going to hit a wall, and it’s going to be public and embarrassing. Right now, the "glue" holding this together is just me and Priya working overtime and David managing relationships. As we approach 30 people, that glue just snaps. You’ll see **total project paralysis**. High-performers hate working in chaos where their work is constantly de-prioritized for the "shiny object of the week." If they feel like they're spinning their wheels and never shipping finished products, they’ll go somewhere that actually has its act together. Once you lose that internal reputation, it’s incredibly hard to get back. We’ll be a 30-person team that costs a lot of money but can't point to any actual impact. **Question:** Imagine we're having this conversation 18 months from now and DI has successfully grown from 10 to 30. What operational problem are you most relieved we solved?
