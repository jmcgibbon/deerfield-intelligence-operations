# Interview with Sarah

> **Objective:** Understand where DI's activities create meaningful compliance risk, whether current controls and ways of working can scale without unnecessarily constraining speed, and how the DI ↔ Compliance operating model should evolve.

The team is also really patient about explaining the technical side of things to me since, I’m a lawyer, not an engineer. But if I'm being real, the collaboration usually happens way too late in the game. I often don't find out about a new project or a data vendor until they’re basically ready to launch, which leads to a lot of last-minute scrambling on my end to make sure we aren't breaking any SEC rules or privacy laws. It’s definitely something I’m hoping a new Director of Ops can help us smooth out.

**Question:** From your perspective, what works well today?

**Question:** Where do you see friction or gaps?

**Answer:** What works well is the culture David has built—there’s a real respect for the rules, and the engineers are actually really collaborative when we do sit down together. They don't talk down to me, which I appreciate. The friction, though, usually comes down to visibility and documentation. Like, last quarter they built this cool AI tool for physician sentiment, but I didn't hear a peep about it until the finish line, so I had to scramble through the privacy and vendor reviews. Plus, when I ask for things like access control lists or data flow diagrams, they often just... don't exist yet. It makes it tough to feel "exam-ready" for the SEC if they were to walk in tomorrow.

Right now, I can sort of manage by chasing people down, but once you triple the headcount, that informal approach is going to break. The biggest thing that won't scale is the lack of a standard framework for things like vendor due diligence and portfolio company deployments. If every single project is a custom negotiation and I'm still finding out about new data sources after they've already been integrated, we’re going to run into a serious regulatory wall. We really need to move away from "it's all in someone's head" to actual documented processes before the team gets any bigger.

**Question:** Is there anything that isn't causing pain yet, but you think will if we don't address it soon?

**Answer:** AI model governance is the big one that keeps me up at night. Right now, the ML engineers are brilliant and they know their models technically, but we don't have a formal inventory or a way to track things like bias, training data sources, or performance monitoring over time. The SEC is getting really aggressive about how firms use AI in investment decisions, and if they ask us to explain our governance framework today, I’d be pointing at a blank whiteboard. Also, our handling of personal data—like PHI or PII from healthcare providers—is something we need to tighten up immediately. It’s manageable with a small team, but as we ingest more data, the risk of a privacy slip-up grows exponentially. We need to bake those protocols in now before it becomes a headline-level problem.

**Question:** That covers the big buckets for sure. If I had to add one specific nuance, maybe we could touch on **third-party dependencies**. We're using a lot of external AI tools and specialized APIs, and I'm often in the dark about what sensitive data is actually being sent out to them or what those confidentiality terms look like. It ties into risk and controls, but it's its own beast. Other than that, your list hits all my pain points—especially the "ways of working" part, because that’s where the day-to-day friction really lives. Where do you want to start?

The team is fast-moving, so they’ll find a new data vendor or a specialized AI API that helps them solve a problem, and they just... start using it. We ended up having to rip it out entirely after it was already integrated, which was a huge waste of everyone's time. If there had been a standard due diligence checklist at the start— "where did this come from, is it legally sourced, what are the privacy terms"—we could have avoided that mess. Currently, there's no central gatekeeper, so I'm often playing catch-up.

I’d love for the Ops person to own the *process*—like, making sure the checklist actually gets filled out and the right people (Legal, IT, Compliance) are looped in before a contract is signed. I don't want to be a bottleneck, but I’d much rather spend thirty minutes looking at a vendor's privacy policy upfront than have to tell the engineers to delete a month's worth of work because the data was sourced illegally.

## Risk & controls

**Question:** What types of DI activity create the greatest compliance or regulatory risk?

**Answer:** The biggest one is definitely anything that touches **investment decisions**. If the Intelligence team builds a model that signals "buy" or "sell" to our traders, we have to be able to explain exactly how that model works to the SEC. If it’s a "black box" with no documentation on its limitations or how it was trained, that’s a massive regulatory red flag. The other huge risk area is **Information Barriers**. When the team produces research that's investment-relevant, we have to be incredibly careful about who sees it and when. If that info leaks to the wrong person before it's properly handled, we’re looking at potential insider trading issues. Plus, as I mentioned, the **PHI and PII** in our healthcare data sources is a ticking time bomb if we don't have airtight access controls. It's a lot of surface area to cover without a formal framework.

**Question:** How well do you think those risks are understood and managed by DI today?

**Question:** I think they *understand* the risks in a high-level, intellectual way—David and the senior guys definitely get that we’re a regulated entity and they aren't trying to be reckless. But there’s a gap between "understanding the risk" and "operationalizing the control." For example, on information barriers, we have the policies on paper, but I’m not sure the engineers have translated those into their actual daily workflows. And with the AI models, the team knows their stuff technically, but the concept of "model governance" as a regulatory requirement just isn't on their radar yet. They’re focused on "does it work?" while I’m focused on "can we prove why it did what it did to an auditor?" Right now, the management of these risks is way too informal—it relies on people doing the right thing rather than a system that ensures it.

**Question:** Are there risks that you think the team may not fully appreciate today?

**Answer:** I think the **portfolio company deployments** are a major blind spot. When the team builds a tool and hands it off to a port-co, there’s this fuzzy sense that "it’s their problem now," but it’s not that simple. If we’re providing the tech and the data, the lines of liability and regulatory responsibility get really blurry. We don't have a standard framework for those deals yet, so we're basically winging it on every deployment. The other one is **informal access controls**. The engineers will tell me, "Oh, we know who has access to that folder," but in a regulatory exam, "trust me" doesn't count. I’ve asked for an access control matrix several times and it just hasn't materialized. They might not realize that the SEC doesn't just care *if* we have controls, they care if we can *prove* we have them and that they're being monitored. It’s that jump from "doing it" to "documenting it" that they haven't quite embraced yet.

**Question:** As DI scales its team, products and use of AI, where do you expect the risk profile to change?

**Answer:** The risk profile is going to shift from "oops, we forgot to document this" to "we have a systemic failure." As we move from 10 to 30 people, the **insider trading and information barrier** risks explode. With more people having access to sensitive research, the chance of someone inadvertently acting on non-public info goes way up if we don't have automated, documented controls. Also, the **AI model risk** changes fundamentally as we move from experiments to production. If we have dozens of models influencing millions of dollars in trades, the "model drift" or bias isn't just a technical quirk—it's a fiduciary risk. If the SEC sees us scaling AI without a formal governance framework—meaning an inventory, periodic reviews, and clear accountability—they'll see it as us being asleep at the wheel. Basically, the margin for error disappears as the complexity grows.

**Question:** Are there areas where the current controls are insufficient relative to the underlying risk?

**Question:** Definitely. **Access controls** are the most glaring one. Right now, it’s way too informal. I’ve been asking for a formal access control matrix for a while now, and it just hasn't happened. If the SEC asks me tomorrow, "Who exactly had access to this physician sentiment data on March 12th?" I honestly couldn't give them a definitive, documented answer. That’s a huge gap. The other big one is **Model Governance**. We’re using AI to influence investment decisions, but we have zero formal documentation on how these models are trained, what their biases are, or how we’re monitoring them for "drift" over time. In my world, if it isn't documented, it didn't happen. We're essentially flying blind from a regulatory standpoint there, even if the tech itself is brilliant. We need to bridge that gap between "the code works" and "the control is evidenced."

**Question:** Are there areas where the current controls are more burdensome than the underlying risk warrants? Or more burdensome as DI is required to follow the same procedures as the broader investment firm?

**Answer:** That's a great question. Honestly, I think the friction usually goes the other way—the team finds *any* control burdensome because they're used to moving at startup speed. But if I'm being fair, some of the broader Deerfield IT and data infrastructure policies can feel a bit "square peg, round hole" for a specialized tech unit like Intelligence. For example, some of the standard firm-wide procurement or software approval steps are designed for buying off-the-shelf stuff like Excel or Bloomberg, not for an engineer who needs to spin up a specific Python library or a niche API for a 48-hour experiment. When we force them into those slow-moving corporate pipes, they just end up working around them, which is the worst-case scenario for me. I’d love to see an Ops person help us build a "fast track" for DI—something that keeps the guardrails in place but doesn't make them wait three weeks for a signature just to test a new tool.

## How Compliance gets engaged

**Question:** At a high level, how does DI know when and how to engage Compliance today?

**Answer:** Right now, it’s mostly based on "vibes" and David’s intuition. There isn't a formal trigger or an intake process. Usually, someone on the team just realizes, "Oh, Sarah might need to see this," and they'll shoot me an email or mention it in passing. The problem is that engineers don't always know what they don't know. They might think, "This is just public social media data, no big deal," without realizing that scraping it or storing it might trigger specific privacy or vendor terms. So, engagement today is basically reactive—I’m brought in to put out fires or do a final check, rather than being part of the planning. It’s definitely not a system I’d call "reliable" as we scale.

**Question:** Are the triggers for Compliance involvement clear and consistently understood?

**Answer:** Not at all. It’s very "I know it when I see it," which is a dangerous way to run a compliance program. The engineers are focused on solving technical problems, so they don't always recognize that a new data scraping technique or a specific AI prompt might have legal or regulatory teeth. There’s no clear "If X, then call Sarah" list. For example, they might not realize that a new vendor contract needs a specific privacy rider, or that a model used for a portfolio company needs a different set of disclosures than one used internally. Right now, I’m relying on them to have a "compliance spidey-sense," and honestly, that’s not their job—it should be a built-in part of the workflow. That’s why I’m so keen on having an Ops person who can act as that filter.

**Question:** Does Compliance generally get involved at the right point, or are you sometimes brought in too early/late?

**Question:** Where, if anywhere, does the current approach create avoidable work or delays for either team?

**Answer:** It’s the "last-minute scramble" that kills us. When I'm brought in at the 11th hour, I often have to ask for changes that would have been easy to implement during the design phase but are a nightmare once the code is written. Take that physician sentiment AI project—if I’d been in the room when they were picking the data provider, I could have flagged the contract issues then. Instead, they built the whole thing, and I had to put the brakes on right before launch to fix the legal paperwork. It makes me look like the "no" person and makes them feel like I’m moving the goalposts. It’s totally avoidable work. If we had an intake process where I could flag considerations early, we’d save weeks of back-and-forth and a lot of gray hair on both sides.

## Scaling the control model

**Question:** What works at 10 people that you don't think will work at 30?

**Answer:** The "direct access" model is definitely going to break. Right now, I can mostly keep tabs on things by just being friendly and catching people in the hallway or on Slack, but you can't "hallway track" 30 people. The informal communication that David and I have is great, but it doesn't scale to a team three times this size. Also, the lack of documentation will go from a "nuance" to a "crisis." With 10 people, you can usually find the one person who knows how a specific model was trained. With 30, that tribal knowledge gets spread too thin. If we don't have a centralized model inventory and clear data flow diagrams, I won't be able to provide the oversight the SEC expects. We’ll basically lose the ability to see the "big picture" of our own risk because there will be too many moving parts for one person to track manually.

**Question:** As activity increases, which controls or reviews need to become more standardized or embedded into the way DI works?

**Question:** The **vendor onboarding process** has to be the first thing we standardize. We can't have engineers just swiping a credit card or signing up for APIs without a central gatekeeper checking the data sourcing and privacy terms. It needs to be a "one-way in" process where Compliance, Legal, and Security all sign off before the data even hits our environment. Second, we need **automated documentation for model governance**. As the number of AI models grows, we can't rely on me asking for updates. We need a standard template for every model: what’s the purpose, what’s the training data, what are the limitations, and who is the owner? If that’s not embedded in the "definition of done" for the engineers, it just won't happen, and I'll be left with a bunch of black boxes that I can't explain to a regulator.

**Question:** Which areas genuinely require Compliance judgment versus potentially being handled through clearer guardrails or standard processes?

**Question:** That's a really smart way to look at it. Honestly, about 70% of what I do right now could be "guardrailed" if we had the right processes. Things like **vendor due diligence** or **access requests** don't always need my specific JD—if we have a clear checklist that says "no scraping PII" and "contracts must have X indemnity clause," an Ops person or even a standardized workflow could handle the first 90% of that. Same with **documentation standards**; I don't need to judge *if* a model should be documented, I just need the Ops team to ensure the documentation exists and meets the firm's template. Where I really need to spend my "judgment" time is on the **novel stuff**. For example, if we’re deploying a new AI tool to a portfolio company and the liability split is tricky, or if we’re using a data source in a way that’s never been done before—that’s where I need to sit down and think through the fiduciary and regulatory implications. If an Ops person can handle the "is the paperwork in order?" side of things, I can actually focus on the high-level risk strategy.

**Question:** What should deliberately remain lightweight?

**Answer:** I think the **early-stage experimentation** needs to stay as lightweight as possible. I don't want to be the reason an engineer can't spend a Saturday afternoon testing a crazy new idea in a sandbox. If they're just playing with public, non-sensitive data to see if a concept has legs, I don't need a 10-page impact assessment. We should keep the "discovery phase" fast and loose. Also, **internal technical documentation**—like the actual code comments or how they structure their databases—should stay with them. I don't need to review how they write their Python scripts. I just need the high-level "what does this do and where does the data come from" summary. As long as the *outputs* and the *data sources* are governed, I’m happy to stay out of the weeds of their day-to-day dev work. We just need to agree on where "playing around" ends and "building for the firm" begins.

## DI ↔ Compliance ways of working

**Question:** How do Compliance and DI coordinate today—are there regular forums/cadences or is the relationship primarily ad hoc?

**Answer:** It’s almost entirely ad hoc right now. There isn't a standing meeting or a formal "Compliance x Intelligence" forum. Usually, it’s just me reaching out to David or one of the engineers when I hear a whisper of something new, or them tagging me in a Slack thread when they realize they might have a legal question. I’m basically operating on a "pull" model—I have to go out and pull information from the team. I’d much rather have a "push" model where there’s a regular cadence, maybe a bi-weekly sync with an Ops lead, where I get a preview of the roadmap. Right now, without that structure, I’m constantly worried I’m missing something simply because I wasn't in the right room at the right time. I'd love to see a **bi-weekly sync** with the Director of Ops. I don't need to take up the engineers' time, but I need one person who can walk me through the roadmap and say, "Here’s what’s moving from sandbox to production," or "We’re looking at these three new data vendors." Beyond that, we need a formal **intake process**. Whether it's a simple Jira ticket or a brief form, there should be a trigger whenever a new data source is considered or a model is being prepped for investment use. If I can get a "heads up" at the start of a sprint rather than at the finish line, I can provide guidance in real-time. It turns me from a "final hurdle" into a "thought partner," which is way better for everyone's stress levels.

**Question:** Do you have the right counterparts, information and visibility from DI to support the team effectively?

**Answer:** not yet. David is a great partner in terms of intent, but he’s spread thin, and the engineers are—rightly—focused on the tech. I’m missing a true "translator" counterpart. I need someone who understands the technical roadmap well enough to spot the compliance tripwires before I even see them. As for visibility, it’s pretty opaque. I’m often looking at the team through a keyhole. I don't have access to their project management tools or a clear view of the data inventory, so I’m relying on what people remember to tell me. To be effective, I need to be "in the loop" by design, not by accident. Having an Ops person who ensures I have the right documentation and a seat at the table during planning would change everything.

**Question:** Where, if anywhere, is ownership or decision-making between Compliance and DI unclear?

**Question:** The biggest gray area is **data ownership and due diligence**. Sometimes the team thinks that if a vendor is already being used by another part of Deerfield, it’s "pre-approved" for them. But the *use case* matters—using a data set for general research is very different from plugging it into an automated trading model. There’s often confusion about who is responsible for verifying those specific usage rights. Another one is **model accountability**. If an AI model produces a biased or incorrect output that leads to a bad investment, is that a "technical bug" for the engineers to fix, or a "compliance failure" that I need to report? We haven't really defined who "owns" the risk of the model's behavior once it's live. We need to clear up those boundaries so we aren't pointing fingers if something actually goes sideways.It’s exactly what we’ve been talking about—**early visibility and a structured intake process**. If I had to pick one thing for a new Ops person to fix on day one, it would be creating a "Compliance & Risk" trigger in their workflow. I’m not looking to slow them down, but I need a way to see what’s coming so I can flag the big stuff—like PII issues or vendor sourcing—before they’ve spent a month building on top of it. If an Ops lead can be my eyes and ears inside the team and make sure the basic documentation (like that access matrix!) actually gets done, it would take the relationship from reactive and stressful to proactive and sustainable. I want to help them build something great, I just need to be in the loop to do it!

**Question:** As DI grows, what should change about how the two teams coordinate—for example, cadence, intake, information sharing or escalation?

**Answer:** As we scale, we have to move away from the "DM Sarah on Slack" model. It just won't work with 30 people. First, the **intake process** needs to be formalized. I’d love to see a "Compliance Check" baked into their project lifecycle—maybe a simple set of questions in Jira or whatever they use for sprint planning. If they check a box saying "uses new data" or "influences trading," it should automatically trigger a notification to me. Second, the **information sharing** needs to be centralized. I shouldn't have to hunt for a data flow diagram; there should be a shared repository where the "Compliance Version" of their documentation lives. Finally, we need a clear **escalation path**. Right now, if I have a concern, I just talk to David. But as the team grows, we need a defined way to handle disagreements—like if I flag a data vendor as high-risk but the team thinks it's mission-critical. Having the Director of Ops as the first point of escalation to mediate those "speed vs. safety" trade-offs would be huge. It keeps the relationship professional and ensures we aren't just hitting a wall every time a tough call needs to be made.

**Question:** Where would an Operations leader within DI add the most value? Where would you not want Operations involved?

**Question:** The Director of Ops would be a total game-changer for me if they acted as the **"Compliance Translator."** Their biggest value would be operationalizing the boring but critical stuff—building that vendor onboarding workflow, chasing down the engineers for the access control matrix, and making sure model documentation actually exists. If they can own the *process* of getting me what I need, it frees me up to be a strategic partner instead of a nag. I also see them adding huge value in **Portfolio Company deployments**. Right now, every one of those is a custom headache. If an Ops leader can build a standard framework or "playbook" for how we hand off tech to port-cos, it would save me (and Legal) dozens of hours of repetitive work. Where would I **not** want them involved? I don’t need them to be a middleman for technical deep-dives. If I have a specific question about how a transformer model is handling data, I still want to talk directly to the engineer who built it. I don't want Ops to become a "game of telephone" where the technical nuance gets lost. And honestly, I don't want them trying to do the legal interpretation themselves—I’m happy to handle the "is this okay under SEC rules?" part, as long as they provide the facts I need to make that call.

## Prioritization

**Question:** Of everything we've discussed, what are the one or two compliance-related operational issues you think matter most? Why those versus the others?

**Answer:** If I have to narrow it down to the absolute "must-haves," it’s **early visibility/intake** and **model governance.** The **early visibility** is number one because it’s the root cause of almost all our friction. If I’m not in the loop until the finish line, I’m forced to be a bottleneck, which everyone hates. It’s the difference between me saying, "Hey, let's tweak this data contract now," and me saying, "We have to delay the launch by three weeks because this vendor is a regulatory nightmare." One is a partnership; the other is a disaster. **Model governance** is the second because it’s our biggest unmanaged regulatory risk. We’re moving fast with AI, and the SEC is looking for a reason to make an example out of someone in this space. If we can’t show a clear inventory and documentation of how our models work and how we monitor them, we’re essentially indefensible in an audit. Everything else—like access controls and vendor checklists—is important, but these two are the ones that could actually stop the business in its tracks if we get them wrong.

**Question:** What would happen if we did nothing about them over the next 12–18 months? What, if anything, would break as DI approaches 30 people?

**Answer:** If we do nothing, we’re basically playing regulatory Russian roulette. As the team hits 30 people, the "hallway track" communication will completely fail, and I guarantee we’ll have a major "oops" moment—either we’ll launch a product using data we don't actually have the rights to use, or we’ll have a significant information barrier breach because no one is tracking who has access to what anymore. The most likely thing to break is our relationship with the SEC. If they come in for an exam and see a team of 30 people deploying sophisticated AI models with zero formal governance or documentation, they won't just see it as a minor oversight—they’ll see it as a systemic failure of our fiduciary duty. We’d be looking at potential fines, forced shutdowns of certain systems, and massive reputational damage. Basically, we’d go from being a "cutting-edge innovator" to a "regulatory cautionary tale" pretty quickly. It’s just not a sustainable way to grow.

**Question:** Imagine we're having this conversation 18 months from now and DI has successfully grown from 10 to 30. What operational problem are you most relieved we solved?

**Answer:** I’d be most relieved that we finally solved the **"Black Box" problem**—both for our AI models and our data flows. If we're at 30 people and I can pull up a central dashboard or inventory that shows exactly what models we’re running, where their data comes from, and who has access to them, I’ll sleep so much better. It would mean we moved past the era of "it's in someone's head" and actually built a professional, defensible infrastructure. Honestly, just knowing that I’m not going to be blindsided by a "surprise launch" on a Friday afternoon ever again would be a huge weight off my shoulders. It would mean the Director of Ops did their job and turned compliance from a frantic fire-drill into a standard, quiet part of how we build things. That’s the dream!

**Question:** If I told you we weren't going to solve that problem, what workaround would you recommend? How long would that workaround remain viable?

**Answer:** If you told me we weren't going to fix the process, my only real workaround would be to get **extremely intrusive.** I’d have to start inserting myself into every Slack channel, every stand-up, and every engineering demo just to play detective. I’d basically have to become a "Compliance Shadow," which the engineers would absolutely hate, and honestly, it’s a terrible use of my JD. That might keep us afloat for another few months while the team is small, but it’s totally **unviable** once you hit 20 or 30 people. I can't be in five places at once. Eventually, I’d miss something big, or the team would get so frustrated with me "snooping" that they’d start hiding things even more. If we don't build a system to handle this, the only other "workaround" is to significantly slow down the pace of innovation—basically saying we can't launch anything new until I've done a manual, painful audit of every single line of data. Nobody wants that.

**Question:** Which of the issues we've discussed today do you think others at DI or Deerfield might see differently from you?

**Answer:** I think the engineers might see **documentation** and **access controls** differently. To them, a formal access matrix or a model governance document probably feels like "bureaucracy for the sake of bureaucracy"—a distraction from actually writing code and solving hard problems. They know their systems work, so they might see my request for a paper trail as a lack of trust, rather than a regulatory necessity. Also, the broader Deerfield IT or Legal teams might see the **"fast-track" for DI experiments** differently. They’re used to protecting the whole firm with one-size-fits-all rules, so they might be wary of giving DI a "special" process for testing new tools. They might see it as opening a backdoor, whereas I see it as a way to keep the team from going rogue. Balancing those two perspectives—the team's need for speed and the firm's need for consistency—is going to be one of the biggest challenges for this new Ops role.
